Autonomy is the single most consequential setting in a birth. It decides whether a prediction becomes advice, a draft, an action, or an incident.

## The ladder

| Level | Name | What the agent does | Human role | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **L0** | Observe | Watches, records, says nothing | None | Instrumentation, data collection |
| **L1** | Suggest | Proposes a prediction or action | Decides everything | Early forecasting, advisory tools |
| **L2** | Draft | Prepares the full action for approval | One approve/reject per action | Email, code change, ticket response |
| **L3** | Act with review | Acts, then reports; a human can reverse | Review afterwards | Reversible writes, internal ops |
| **L4** | Act within scope | Acts freely inside a named scope and budget | Exception handling | Established workflows, R1 tools |
| **L5** | Act, report | Acts at its own discretion, reports on a cadence | Audit only | Only where the cost of error is bounded and known |

Two rules that are not negotiable:

**Autonomy is never above the contract.** An L5 label does not authorise an R3 action; irreversible actions always require an explicit gate. See [Tools](/births/tools) for the risk classes.

**Autonomy is per action class, not per agent.** A support agent might be L5 for tagging, L3 for refunds under £50 and L2 for anything above. One number for a whole agent invites the wrong kind of trust.

## Earning the level

Autonomy is granted on evidence, in a fixed order:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Shadow (L0–L1)">
    The agent predicts alongside a human deciding. Nobody acts on it. You are measuring calibration and surprise.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Draft (L2)">
    Accuracy is acceptable and the human is mostly clicking approve. You are measuring how often the human changes the answer. That rate is the real cost of the agent.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Reviewed action (L3–L4)">
    Reversibility is high and the review rate has fallen. You are measuring exceptions.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Unsupervised (L5)">
    Only where the blast radius is bounded by construction: a budget cap, an irreversible-action gate and a kill switch all in place.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Moving a level up is a decision with a name attached. Moving it down is always allowed, immediately, by anyone with the authority over that scope. A demotion that requires a meeting will not happen at 3am when it is needed.

## Budgets are part of autonomy

An agent with L5 and no budget is not autonomous. It is unbounded. Every contract carries:

- **Spend**: money per run and per day.
- **Time**: wall clock, and what happens on timeout.
- **Iterations**: how many attempts before it must escalate.
- **Blast radius**: how many entities a single run may touch.

When the budget runs out the agent stops and escalates. It does not degrade into an unaudited mode, and it does not "just finish" the last step.

## Escalation

Escalation is a first-class action, not an error. An agent escalates when:

- the situation is outside its remit,
- the action it needs is a higher risk class than its contract allows,
- confidence is below the threshold its contract names,
- the cost of being wrong exceeds the value of being fast.

A well-designed agent escalates early and cheaply. An agent that escalates never is not confident; it is out of control, and you find out at the consequence rather than at the decision.

</Note>
